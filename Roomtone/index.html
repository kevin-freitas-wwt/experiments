Roomtone · an interface that listens

This page is reading the room.

Not your clicks. Not your scroll. The air. Sit in silence and it opens up. Start typing and it gets out of the way. Let a voice come near and it quietly covers what's private.

Almost every interface treats input as something you aim at it: a cursor, a keystroke, a tap. That leaves out nearly everything true about the moment you're in. Whether you're alone. Whether someone just walked up behind you. Whether the room is loud enough that small type has become useless.

A microphone answers all three, and it can answer them without a single byte leaving the machine. Everything below is computed from an AnalyserNode in this tab and thrown away frame by frame. There is no network code on this page at all.

What it hears

The private bit

Here is a line worth hiding: the offer came in at $184,000 and Dana hasn't been told yet. When the classifier decides a voice is in the room, that span blurs and marks itself held. Turn away, keep quiet, and it comes back.

This is the honest version of a pattern that usually gets built badly. Presence detection normally means a camera, a cloud model, and a privacy policy nobody reads. Sound is coarser, which is exactly why it's safer: it can tell that a voice is present without ever needing to know whose.

Where it's wrong

Often. A fan reads as music. A loud typist reads as speech. That's the interesting part rather than the flaw — ambient input can't be correct, so the design has to make being wrong cheap. Every response here is reversible, slow enough to feel like weather, and never destructive. Nothing is submitted, saved, or sent because the room got loud.

The slowness is deliberate. Every transition runs near 900 ms with a long tail, and the classifier holds a state for at least a second and a half before it will hand over. A reactive interface that reacts instantly to a coughing fit is a strobe light, not a tool.

Try it

Go silent for five seconds. Then type. Then talk to someone, or to yourself. Then put music on. The instrument in the corner shows the five features the classifier actually looks at, so you can watch it make up its mind and catch it being wrong.
