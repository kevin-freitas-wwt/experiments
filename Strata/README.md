# Strata

A voxel landscape generated as you walk through it, a solar system above it, and other suns beyond. One seed word decides where you wake up, how the continents lie, how many planets, asteroids, ships and stations the home system has, and the layout of every system you fly to after it. Everything is rendered in more detail the closer you get.

Live: https://kevin-freitas-wwt.github.io/experiments/Strata/

## Running

ES modules and workers need `http://`, not `file://`:

```bash
python3 -m http.server 8765      # from the experiments folder
```

Open http://localhost:8765/Strata/. Everything Strata needs lives in its own folder: Three.js 0.187.0 and the Shippori Mincho font are in `Strata/vendor/` (see the README there for licenses). There is no build step and no network request at all; the folder can be copied anywhere and served as is.

URL parameters: `?seed=word` picks a world, `?at=x,y,z,yaw,pitch,fly` is a place in it (the address bar is rewritten every second as you move, so the current URL is always a shareable link to where you stand; `in=` adds the vessel when you are aboard one, and `on=` adds the body and your body-local position when you stand on a planet or moon, since it will have turned by the time the link is opened), `?r=11` sets the detail radius in chunks (4 to 14; it also scales the heightfield ranges and the distances at which planets and asteroids step up in detail), `?crumb=8` sets breadcrumb spacing in metres, `?hour=18` sets the home sun's phase at load, `?refl=0` disables water reflections, and `?pose=x,y,z,yaw,pitch` frames a still with the overlay hidden (used for the thumbnail).

## How it is made

The world is split into 16×16 columns, 384 blocks tall. Each chunk is built in a Web Worker (a pool of up to four) from nothing but its coordinates and the seed:

1. **Column heights.** A low-frequency value-noise FBM (fractal Brownian motion) decides continent vs. sea; a ridged multifractal adds mountains only where the continent term is strong; a short-wavelength FBM adds roll. The spawn point is drawn from the seed (within 12 km of the origin), and the continent field is offset so that point always lands a few blocks above sea level near a coast. The home world is a disc: beyond about 100 km from spawn the land sinks into deep sea, the heightfield tiles stop at 170 km, and the sea disc ends at 190 km, so from another system it reads as a small flat world rather than an infinite plane.
2. **Biomes.** Temperature (noise minus altitude) and moisture (noise) pick one of nine terrains: open sea, shore, plains, forest, dry basin, tundra, taiga, highlands, snowfield. Each has its own surface stack and tree density.
3. **Trees.** A per-column hash places broadleaf canopies or conifers. The worker generates a 3-block apron around every chunk so trees straddling a boundary are complete on both sides.
4. **Meshing.** Only faces that touch air or water are emitted, greedily merged into larger quads wherever colour and ambient-occlusion pattern allow (about 3× fewer triangles in forest, far more on open ground), each with a palette colour, a face shade and baked vertex ambient occlusion (the classic 0-to-3 corner count); the per-block tone jitter is applied in the fragment shader from the block's cell so merged faces still read as blocks. Water is a single sheet of top faces, drawn translucent in a second pass with an animated ripple shader (scrolling waves bend the normal and add sparkle in world xz, so it reads the same at home and on any planet shell) and a planar reflection: each frame the scene is rendered again at half size from a camera mirrored in the liquid plane of the frame you stand in (the home sea, or a shell's sea level), with everything below the plane clipped and the water itself hidden, and the water samples that image through its ripples, blended by a Fresnel term biased high so land, clouds, moons and sky show even when you look down at it (`?refl=0` turns the pass off). Positions, colours, normals and indices come back as transferable buffers.

The main thread batches chunks into 4×4 super-chunks (one mesh per material, rebuilt when a member arrives or leaves, so a 14-chunk ring is a few dozen draw calls rather than six hundred; each vertex carries the second its chunk arrived and the shader dithers it in from there, and a per-world uniform dithers a whole retiring shell out). It keeps a ring of chunks around the player, generating the ones in front of you first (priority is distance weighted by the angle from your heading, re-ranked when you turn; heightfield tiles use the same rule), installs at most three new chunks per frame, and drops anything more than two chunks outside the ring. Above 700 m the ring stops following you; the ground beyond it is carried by three levels of heightfield tiles from the same column function (256 m tiles at 8 m steps out to 3 km, 1 km tiles at 32 m steps out to 12 km, 4 km tiles at 128 m steps out to 40 km), each tile hidden once the finer level fully covers it, with a plain sea disc under everything. There is no fog. Instead a vertex shader drops every ground vertex by d²/2R (R = 90 km), so a horizon appears as you climb and the flat world reads as a small planet from orbit.

## Space

Past 1.5 km the sky thins to black and stars fade in. The home system has two or three planets (other systems one to four) drawn from five kinds (lush, icy, rocky, ember, and past the frost line gas giants), with radii on a rough power law from 1.3 km to 100 km (home-world scale; about one in eight is over 40 km) and orbits spaced at least six summed radii plus 40 km apart on a geometric ladder (ratio 2 to 2.3) out to 1200 km (the home system keeps one showpiece, a 20 to 30 km ringed world 300 km out; temperature, and so kind, is taken from the distance to the home sun), each an icosphere displaced and coloured by 3D noise in a worker at four detail levels (320 to 327,680 triangles) chosen by apparent triangle size (the top level is skipped on worlds under 20 km while their heightfield tiles are up, and levels more than one step from the current one are released), and within 1.6 radii a 97×97 high-resolution patch of the same surface function is regenerated under you as you move. Below about 4 km (scaled with the detail radius, capped at half the planet's radius) a ring of 256 m heightfield tiles at 8 m steps is laid on the sphere around you, out to 3 km: the tiles are exact sphere points keyed on a tangent-frame grid that is re-framed as you drift, dithered away where the voxels cover them, with the smooth sphere dithered away beneath them (`pfar.js`). Within 220 m of the ground the planet becomes voxels: a tangent-plane shell (the same chunk pipeline as the home world, fed by the planet's surface function through a gnomonic projection, with column heights following the true sphere) is attached to the planet with its own frame, so gravity points at the planet's centre and you can land, walk and jump. The shell re-centres when you have moved about 7% of the planet's radius from its origin (64 m on the smallest worlds, up to 400 m on the largest); the old shell stays up until the new one has loaded and then dissolves under it, so nothing blinks. It dissolves the same way when you climb above 350 m. Lush worlds get grass, forest and seas (the voxel shell carves the seabed from the same height field, capped 14 blocks deep, and fills to sea level with water, ice or lava); icy ones snow over ice with frozen seas and the odd conifer; ember worlds basalt and rust around lava seas; rocky ones stone and gravel highlands and dry basins. Lush and icy worlds have atmospheres, and with them clouds and haze. A cloud shell sits above the highest peaks: its cover (a tenth to two thirds of the sky, seeded per world) comes from two scales of 3D value noise, weather systems and cumulus texture, drifting on a wind, lit by the world's star, grey underneath and fading as you fly through it; the same noise runs on the main thread so cloud between you and the star dims the direct light and the lens flare, while the star itself still shows through gaps. Aerial perspective fades everything with distance through an exponential atmosphere (sea-level extinction 4.5e-5 per metre, 2.5 km scale height, integrated along the view ray), so hills a few tens of kilometres off melt into the horizon colour and a planet seen from orbit wears a pale veil rather than showing bare; the veil is lit by the star, so the night side stays dark. Anything beyond the air (planets, moons, rings, other suns) is seen through the whole column of airlight, so by day the sky washes it toward its own colour, more so near the horizon, exactly as the Moon pales in a daytime sky and stands out at night. The atmosphere rim drawn around each such world sits above its cloud shell. Their sky dome takes their colours (blue, or pale steel for ice) oriented to the planet's up, thinning to stars with altitude exactly as at home, and hemisphere light follows. Rocky and ember worlds are airless, so their sky stays black at any height and they grow nothing: no trees, no grass, no tundra. The smooth planet mesh is dithered away under the shell so the two never fight; the hole is sized each frame to the radius the voxel chunks actually cover and have finished fading in, so the lower level of detail stays underneath until the voxels are solid and you never see through the surface, and it shrinks again when you leave, with the voxels fading, rather than switching. The smooth sphere, the high-res patch and the voxel shell share one surface function (`planet.js`) for height, biome and colour, the 3D noise is normalised so extra octaves only add detail, and the smooth colours are dimmed to match voxel ambient occlusion, so the three agree in topography and tone to within a block. Columns are 384 blocks tall and each planet shell picks its own vertical origin by sampling the surface across the ring it will cover (plus the sea level), so the lowest valley sits 30 blocks above the bottom and the highest peak fits beneath the top; nothing is flattened into a plateau unless local relief exceeds 370 m, which the terrain amplitudes do not reach. Detail changes everywhere cross-fade with hashed alpha rather than popping, and the seams between voxel ring, fine tiles and coarse tiles are dithered over a band rather than cut. Rings are uncommon (the first home planet always has them, outer worlds about one in five, inner ones one in twenty, moons never): one to four lanes, each with its own inner and outer radius, thickness, density, opacity, banding frequency and tint, separated by seeded gaps. From a distance a lane is a shader disc with bands and gaps; within about 7 km the disc fades out and the ring resolves into particles generated per 2 km cell of the ring plane (hundreds per cell, sizes on a power law up to 45 m, scattered through the lane's thickness), low-poly instances at range and voxel rocks within 900 m, with their own gravity like any asteroid. Planets over 8 km carry up to three moons (a twentieth to a sixth of their size, orbiting beyond the rings once every 10 to 40 minutes, surface speed capped at 60 m/s): they are ordinary bodies, with the same detail levels, tiles, voxel shells and gravity, so you can land on one and watch its planet cross the sky. Most moons are tidally locked, keeping one face to their host as they go round; a third ride eccentric orbits (r = a(1 - e²)/(1 + e cos θ), e up to 0.35), sweeping equal areas in equal times so they hurry past periapsis and linger far out. About one world in eight over 5 km is a double planet: a companion more than half its size, close in and locked, with any moons beyond it (the system list calls it a twin). A gas giant is about half of the cold worlds over 25 km: a rocky core (its `radius`) under three banded, near-opaque decks of sickly yellow cloud reaching 1.6 to 2.1 core radii (its visible `size`, which is what orbits, moons, rings, travel and the system list are measured against), each deck seeded and phased on its own so their gaps never line up, drawn as outer faces from outside and undersides from within so a shell's far side never paints over the layers inside it. The air between is a deep haze (scale height a third of the deck depth, extinction 1e-4 per metre), so the descent is murk, deck after deck, until the dim ember core shows: basalt and rust around lava seas, walkable like any other world, with the cloud base as its sky. Thirty to 220 free asteroids, most of them in a belt around the ringed or biggest body, are low-poly instances until you come within a few kilometres, when each is generated as a voxel blob and meshed with the same ambient-occlusion mesher as the ground.

## The cosmos

The cosmos is a seeded barred-spiral galaxy 15 million km across (`galaxy.js`): a density function with two log-spiral arms, a bar and bulge, a thin disc, a sparse halo and four dozen globular clusters, tilted 62° to the home ground so its band arcs over the sky. The home world sits 55% of the way out along one arm. Space is divided into 2400 km cells and each cell holds a system with a probability set by the density there (about one in two near home, nearly all in the bulge, almost none in the halo), so flying coreward the suns crowd and brighten and flying out they thin away. The night sky is a 17,000-point sample of the same density, re-aimed from wherever you are whenever you have moved 1% of the galaxy, so the band, the bulge and the clusters stand where the systems really are and swing round as you cross an arm. Inside a system flight tops out at 500 km/s; clear of the outer planets the cap climbs quickly toward 3000 km/s and the boost winds up three times faster, so a neighbouring sun is a second or two away, and the survey card shows the light-time to the nearest body and system.


Each system has a star (white, yellow, orange or blue), one to four planets, some ringed, its own belt, four to twenty ships and up to five stations, all from the cell's hash. Systems within about 5000 km of you exist (a dozen or so near home, the nearest around 3000 km off); the rest are points of the sky until you get there. Flight speed keeps scaling with clearance from the nearest body or star, with no practical cap, so a neighbouring system is a few seconds away at full boost, and the same rule slows you on arrival. To keep float precision, the whole scene is re-based around the camera every 50 km of travel; positions you see in the HUD are relative to home. The Travel list and the number keys always refer to the nearest system.

Asteroids have their own gravity: within a few radii of a loaded one you fall toward its centre, land on whichever face is below you and walk around it, with the frame re-oriented every step so up is always away from the middle. Thirteen procedural voxel ships in three classes (skiffs, mid-size hulls with wings, and capital ships with a bridge tower and four engines, up to 650 m long) orbit the planets, and three ring stations, each generated from a torus, a hub, spokes and solar panels, orbit the inner three. Every ship and station has an airlock, marked by an orange door and a pale beacon on the hull; fly within 45 m and press `Enter` to board a procedurally generated interior (ships: a spine corridor with cabins, an engine room and a glazed bridge; stations: a hub with a reactor, spokes and a ring corridor with rooms and outward windows), all voxels at 1 m with glass, lights and panels. `Enter` at the door takes you back out. Interiors ride along inside the moving vessel.

Flight speed scales with clearance: 14 m/s on the deck, growing by that much again for every 120 m of height or distance to the nearest planet or star, and a second factor that doubles it per 30 km of clearance. Holding any thrust key in flight builds a boost from 1× to 8× over four seconds (shown in the HUD; it fades within 6 km of anything and drops the moment you let go), `Shift` triples the lot, and the whole thing caps at 500 km/s. Braking is four times stiffer than acceleration, so arriving at a planet from a cross-system run does not overshoot. Space is under a minute away and the same rule slows you down again on approach, so you can land on a planet without overshooting; planets and stars are solid, nothing else is. Movement on the ground is a swept AABB (axis-aligned bounding box) against the block grid: `E S D F` to walk, `Shift` to sprint, `A` to jump or rise, `Z` to descend, `V` to toggle flight. Every lettered key can be rebound from the Controls list on the start card (click a key, press the new one; saved in the browser, `reset` restores the defaults; `src/keys.js`). `Esc` on the start card is the same as Set out; in play it hands the pointer back and the card returns. `B` turns on breadcrumbs, bright red dots dropped every few metres (spacing from the start card or `?crumb=`) so a route can be retraced; `X` clears them. `1` to `7` jump to a planet of the nearest system, `8` to the nearest asteroid, `9` to the nearest ship, `-` to the nearest station and `0` home; `Enter` uses an airlock; a left click with the aiming ring on any planet, star, asteroid, ship or station takes you toward it, halving your clearance to its surface with each click until you are set down on it (ships and stations stop at airlock range, stars at a safe standoff); `P` saves a PNG of the view with no overlays (also a button on the start card, next to copy link); the same list sits under Travel on the start card.

## Day and night

Night light comes from what is actually overhead: the galaxy's own glow where you stand (brighter in the bulge, faint in the halo), shine from lit moons and planets above the horizon as a coloured directional light from the brightest of them (phase and angular size, exaggerated well past real moonlight so nights read), and ring shine when you stand on a ringed world.

Light always comes from the nearest star. At home the sun circles the world once every 24 minutes on a great circle through the zenith (`?hour=0` to `24` sets the phase at load; otherwise the seed picks it), and every other planet spins under its own star once every 20 to 50 minutes, so days pass while you walk. The sky dims to a deep blue at night with the star field showing through, warms to orange around the sun at dawn and dusk, and the directional, hemisphere and ambient lights follow the sun's elevation.


## Weather and seasons

The home world lies at 47° north on a planet tilted 23°, so the sun's noon height and the day's length swing with the season (`?day=` 0 to 365, else from the seed; a year passes in thirty home days). Snow follows: a snow line in the terrain shader whitens upward faces above a height that rises in summer and falls in winter (16 to 88 m above the sea at home; on lush planets it tracks the latitude too, lower toward the poles and into whichever hemisphere is having winter), with a noisy, soft edge, so the seasons move the snow without regenerating a block. Rain falls from the dense heart of the cloud overhead (snow where it is cold: icy worlds, or above the snow line): a box of streaks around you, oriented to the local up and animated entirely in the shader, with a hiss in the ambience and the haze thickened. It wets the ground, which darkens and pools sky-coloured puddles on flat tops, then dries over a minute or two once the rain stops. For a while either side of sunrise on clear mornings a second, shallow fog layer (40 m scale height) pools in the valleys and over the sea. At night, auroras hang near the poles of atmosphere worlds: curtains of green shading to violet in a band at 68° latitude above the air, animated and only on the night side; at home they stand as one wall far to the north.

## Water

Rivers wind across the lowlands: the zero crossings of a slow noise, cut a few blocks below the sea where the land is low enough to reach it, with banks tapering over a second band width, so the sea fills them and they run to the coast; lush planets get the same from their surface function, so sphere, tiles and voxels agree. With your eye under the surface the world sinks into the liquid's own murk (a short, even extinction in deep blue-green, darker at night; lava is a glowing red wall a couple of metres deep) and, by day on living worlds, caustics play over the bed: two drifting noise sheets interfering, fading with depth. At night on those seas plankton light up along the ripple crests in slow drifting patches. The planar reflection stands in for refraction; the view through the surface is not bent.

## Ruins

Someone was here first. On any solid ground, seeded and sparse, the chunk generator leaves cairns on high or bare ground (a ring of stone and gravel and a stack two to four high), monoliths (a basalt pillar one or two blocks square and nine to fourteen tall, sunk two into the ground) and, once in a few hundred chunks, a crashed hull: a plate-and-panel wreck seven blocks long, half sunk and torn open toward its tail. In orbit, about a third of the stations beyond the first are dead: dark, holed, missing a wedge of their ring and tumbling slowly, listed as the wreck of the station they were.

## Start card

The start card opens with a gallery of five seeds, each a still rendered from inside that world (`gallery/*.jpg`) and a link straight into it; Travel and Controls fold below.

## Measuring

The survey card (altitude, terrain, position, speed, nearest body, system, loaded counts, mode, crumbs) is a diagnostic view and is hidden by default; `K` toggles it and `?stats=1` starts with it on. With it come frame time and JavaScript time per frame, draw calls, triangles, worker queue depth and GPU geometry counts, read from the renderer at the end of each frame. Numbers worth knowing on a mid detail radius: heightfield tiles, not voxels, were most of the triangle budget until they were frustum-culled (their bounding spheres are padded by the curvature drop), which halved both draw calls and triangles.

## Sound

All of it is synthesised in the browser (`sound.js`): pink noise through a wandering band-pass for a light, steady breeze wherever there is air (it does not rise with speed or height, so flight is quiet) that dies on airless worlds and in vacuum; a bright hiss in the rain; low-passed noise swelling on a slow cycle for surf, heard within a few tens of metres of water; and a filtered sawtooth hum with random ticks aboard a vessel. Space and airless worlds are silent. `M` mutes, remembered per browser.

## Palette

Woodblock-print tones: Prussian-blue water, cream mist on the horizon, ochre sand, muted mosses, a vermilion seed stamp. The HUD (heads-up display) is a serif survey card; the seal in the corner is the numeric seed.

| `src/materials.js` | shared materials, horizon-curvature and edge-dither shader patch, hashed-alpha fade-ins |

| `src/crumbs.js` | breadcrumb trail (one InstancedMesh, unfogged so it reads at distance) |
| `src/keys.js` | key bindings, their saved overrides and the editable Controls list |
| `src/main.js` | scene, sky, lights, HUD, URL parameters |
